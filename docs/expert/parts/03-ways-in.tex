\chapter{The ways in}
\label{ch:ways}

\nodwyn{} has one core and several faces. They all speak to the same
sessions, through the same guard, into the same logs, so you can start a
task in one and answer it from another.

\section{The daemon underneath}

\code{nodwyn daemon} is a small server that owns sessions and speaks a
JSON-RPC protocol over a private Unix socket. The browser console, the
terminal client and \code{nodwyn run} are all clients of it. You rarely start
it by hand: the app starts one inside itself when none is running.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lX}
\toprule
\textbf{command} & \textbf{what you get} \\
\midrule
\code{nodwyn} & a line-mode session in the current folder; attaches to a running daemon or runs its own \\
\code{nodwyn app} & a window (or your browser) with the console; attaches to a running daemon or runs its own \\
\code{nodwyn ui} & the console in your browser, attached to a running daemon \\
\code{nodwyn tui} & a full-screen terminal client; attaches to a running daemon or runs its own \\
\code{nodwyn daemon} & the session server in the foreground \\
\code{nodwyn run \ldots} & carry a task to its end with no conversation, for scripts and benchmarks \\
\code{nodwyn repl}, \code{nodwyn eval} & the Scheme interpreter on its own, no session \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{The console}

The console, in the app's window or a browser tab, is organised around
tasks. \textbf{Home} starts a task in a folder and lists what needs your
answer, what is running and what finished. Every task it starts is a chat
(\adr{35}): a request that takes long is a message like any other, and its
turn goes on until the model answers or a hard limit stops it. A
\textbf{task} shows its conversation, each write with its diff and, when
something asks, the approval inline (the reasons and the buttons from
Chapter~\ref{ch:first}), and beside it the plan, this turn's budget, the
the recorded write approvals, the project notes and the workspace.
In Changes, click a filename or press Enter or Space to expand its latest
approved preview, with line numbers and addition/deletion counts. History's
Compare with current file is a separate read-only comparison from a kept
version to today's file. Open inspectors survive redraws; comparisons refresh
only when requested. Missing previews and empty or oversized diffs say so.
Streamed text and thinking are joined for the next display frame, without
redrawing the heading and detail panels per fragment. A task kept off screen
catches up when shown; replies and questions flush preceding fragments at
once. Reading earlier text keeps your scroll position.
The sidebar lists what needs your answer first, then every other task
under its folder, the folder with the latest activity first. A task is
read by a short title the model writes from its first message until you
name it with the pencil beside its heading (by the first message itself
when the model gives none); a closed task can be archived, out of the list until you bring
it back or resume it, or deleted for good, conversation and log
(\adr{39}).
\textbf{Connections} lists the tools (MCP servers among them), the
credential bindings a task loaded, the named workspaces and the models;
\textbf{Workspaces} edits your named folders; and \textbf{Settings} holds
what a task does without asking you (Approvals: writing files and running
commands), each turn's limits and the spend limit, the models and their
settings, the judge, the context window, the keychain and the paired
devices. A change applies to tasks started afterwards; open ones keep what
they started with.

\begin{tip}
The console never invents state. Budgets are counted from the log, the plan
is the recorded plan, and Changes shows recorded approvals, not proof that
writes succeeded: approval precedes execution, and the tool result records
success or failure. Older records without previews are not reconstructed. If a panel has no
source for something, it says so instead of guessing.
\end{tip}

\section{The terminal client}

\code{nodwyn tui} shows the same conversation full-screen, with a multi-line
composer (\key{Alt+Enter} or \key{Ctrl+J} for a newline, \key{Enter} to
send), and inspectors behind colon commands: \code{:plan}, \code{:budget},
\code{:tools NAME} (a tool's input schema), \code{:bindings},
\code{:replay}, \code{:log [n] [kind]}. \code{:model} shows the
conversation's model and the choices, and \code{:model NAME [EFFORT]} moves
it from its next turn; \code{:attach PATH} adds a file. In a git
repository's top folder the chooser offers a new session in a worktree of its
own first, and \code{-{}-worktree} opens one directly. \key{Ctrl+C} aborts busy
work or an attachment, \key{Ctrl+Q} detaches, \key{Ctrl+R} reconnects, and
\code{:close} closes the task. The TUI is a client of the daemon, as the
console is: it uses the one running, or starts one in its own process (then
quitting ends its tasks), and asks for a locked keychain's passphrase before
its full screen opens. A task open in it stays open to the console and runs
on its schedule.

\section{Runs, for scripts and benchmarks}

A run carries a task to its end with no conversation: it goes on for
several turns without anybody typing each message. It is a batch tool, for
scripts and benchmarks, started from the command line:

\begin{sh}
nodwyn run --task task.md --repo . --max-turns 20 --budget-wall 2h
nodwyn run status SESSION
nodwyn run stop SESSION
nodwyn run resume SESSION
\end{sh}

Every turn of a run must record a plan before anything irreversible
happens; the first drafts it and later turns carry it on, with the steps
marked so far. Between turns you get a \textbf{run checkpoint}: a yes
continues, and grants nothing else. The run ends when the model's final line
is exactly \code{NODWYN\_RUN\_COMPLETE}, which is the model's claim, not a
proof; or when the turn or time limit runs out. With
\code{-{}-unattended} nobody is asked anything, and the run goes on only
after a turn that marked a step of its plan done; with \code{-{}-judge} the
judge answers its ordinary tool confirms. Chapter~\ref{ch:plans} shows the
Scheme that makes this work.

The console does not start or resume runs. It shows a run started
elsewhere, and its Stop ends it; a message afterwards goes on with the
task as a chat.

\section{From another device}

The console can also serve other machines: your phone, or, when nodwyn runs
on a server nobody sits at, every device you use it from:

\begin{sh}
nodwyn ui --listen 127.0.0.1:7777 --name https://nodwyn.example.net  # behind your proxy that ends TLS
nodwyn devices invite                        # an invitation for another device
nodwyn devices                               # who is paired
nodwyn devices revoke phone
\end{sh}

Such a console takes no pairing code. The device opens the address and
enters an invitation, from \code{nodwyn devices invite} or from Invite a
device in Settings on a device already paired, \emph{and a device name}.
Over https an invitation from the terminal, or from a device signed in
with your passkey after a touch of it, also makes a passkey there; your
other devices then sign in with that passkey, with no invitation. One made
in this computer's own browser lets in a device that only answers: making the
person, who may trust a project's startup code, stays with the terminal and
the passkey. That browser's sign-in is a token its page keeps in the
browser's storage for the console's origin and sends itself, not a cookie,
which the browser sent to every server on loopback, a task's dev server among
them. A device stays signed
in for as long as it is used, and only 90 days without use sign it out.
Only hashes of its token and of invitations, and the passkey's public key,
are stored. Every answer from it is recorded with its name, \code{via
web:phone}, and \code{passkey:NAME} when it signed in with one, so the log
says which screen said yes.

Served over https (\code{--tls-cert}, or a proxy that ends TLS with
\code{--name https://NAME}), the console is also an app. On an iPhone,
Safari's Share \textrightarrow{} Add to Home Screen puts it there; on Android,
Chrome's Install app does. It opens full screen and, once Settings
\textrightarrow{} This browser \textrightarrow{} Notifications is on, says
``nodwyn needs your answer'' when a task waits, and nothing more. Its service
worker caches nothing, so no task is kept on the phone, and there is nothing
to update but \nodwyn{} itself. A notice while the app is closed is not
built.

There is no relay in between: the phone talks straight to your computer, so
your computer must be on and reachable, and the network path (a tailnet, or
your home Wi-Fi) is yours to provide. When the path breaks (the phone
sleeps, changes network, or \code{nodwyn ui} restarts) the console connects
again by itself, at once when the page is shown again, and reloads the open
task from its log; nothing that was on its way is sent again. The console
pings an open page every 30 seconds, so a proxy such as \code{tailscale
serve} does not close a quiet connection. A revoked phone goes to pairing
and stops trying. A page that comes back to an upgraded nodwyn (after
\code{make promote}) still runs the code it loaded, so it says so and
offers \textbf{Reload}; it never reloads by itself, since a reply being
written lives only in the page and a reload loses it.

\section{Several hosts in one window}

With \nodwyn{} on a laptop and on a server that is always on, the laptop's
window shows both hosts' tasks (\adr{54}). Hosts \textrightarrow{} Add a host
takes the server's address, the laptop's name there, what to call the
server here and an invitation made there. The laptop's daemon pairs with
the server's console as a device, keeps the device's token in the laptop's
keychain, bound to the server's name like any key, and from then on relays
the page's socket for the server (\code{/socket?host=workshop}) to it, so the
page keeps one origin and never holds the token. The sidebar groups tasks
by host, Needs you holds every host's questions, Home's Runs on chooses
where a new task starts, with that host's folders and model, and a notice
of the server's task opens it there. A link made with an invitation from the
person's passkey device also changes the server's limits and models from the
laptop's Settings, unless the server's console was started with
\code{-{}-no-link-settings}; the rest of its setup stays in its own console.

The server decides what the link may do, as it does for any device: it
answers and starts tasks, and when your device signed in there with your
passkey made its invitation, it makes your choices for the server's tasks
too, until that passkey is removed, and changes its limits and models. It never
changes the rest of the server's setup, since that waits for a touch of a
passkey, which it cannot give: Open its console, on Hosts, is for that.
Revoking it on the server ends the link; Unlink on the laptop forgets it
and asks the server to forget the laptop. Everything for the server passes
the laptop's daemon, so its tasks show while the laptop's \nodwyn{} runs.

\begin{careful}
A paired device can answer approvals, and approving a command \emph{is}
running a command as you. Pair only devices you would hand a terminal. A
device paired without a passkey can never change settings, keys, policy
files or the workspace registry. A device signed in with your passkey can,
after a touch of the passkey that lasts until 15 minutes after its last
change, since the passkey proves it is you: it needs your face, finger or
PIN and never leaves the device. So what asks, Settings
\textrightarrow{} Approvals, is chosen by you, and a device's tasks get
what was chosen.
\end{careful}

\section{Sharing with people who will never open a terminal}

\file{Nodwyn.app} is built for that. It starts its own daemon, pairs its own
window with a one-time ticket in the address's fragment (never sent to a
server, valid for a minute), and walks a first-time user through choosing a
model and creating a keychain. Each person's tasks, keys and settings stay
in their own account. \file{docs/app.md} has the steps for sending it to
someone.
